A short history of AI, part 3: transformers and large language models (2017–2026)

Created on October 22, 2026

2026   ·   artificial-intelligence   history-of-science   large-language-models   deep-learning   ·   notes

Part 1 followed AI from Alan Turing to Deep Blue, and Part 2 from Deep Blue to the Transformer, the attention-based network that Google researchers proposed in June 2017. This last part covers the nine years since then. They contain more change than the previous eighty: language models grew from a research curiosity into tools used by more than a billion people, and from tools into agents that write and run code on their own. It ends with the models of late 2026, including Claude Opus 5.5 and Gemini 4 Argon. After this, the blog turns back to the Earth for a while.

Pre-training: learning language by predicting words (2017–2019)

The Transformer was built for translation, but its real impact came from a different use. In 2018 OpenAI trained a Transformer on a large collection of books simply to predict the next word in a sentence, and then fine-tuned it on specific tasks; this “generative pre-training” (the GPT in ChatGPT) improved results on most of the language-understanding tasks the authors tried (Radford et al. 2018). A few months later Google’s BERT learned by filling in randomly hidden words using the context on both sides, and set new records on a wide range of benchmarks (Devlin et al. 2019).

The idea behind both is that predicting text well requires knowing a lot about the world the text describes. Nobody labels the data: the next word is its own answer, so any text on the internet becomes training material. GPT-2, trained on millions of web pages, could write passages of startling fluency and perform some tasks it had never been trained on, simply because they appeared in its training text (Radford et al. 2019).

The scaling era (2020–2022)

In January 2020 a group at OpenAI reported that the error of a language model falls as a smooth power law as the model, the data and the computing budget grow, with some trends holding over more than seven orders of magnitude (Kaplan et al. 2020). For a field used to unpredictable progress, this was a remarkable finding: it said that bigger would reliably be better, and by how much. Four months later GPT-3, with 175 billion parameters, showed what that meant in practice. Given just a few examples of a task in its prompt, without any further training, it could translate, answer questions or write simple code (Brown et al. 2020).

The race that followed is visible in Figure 2. Model size grew about 2,500-fold in five years, reaching 540 billion parameters with Google’s PaLM (Chowdhery et al. 2022). Then DeepMind showed that most of these models had been trained on too little data for their size: Chinchilla, with 70 billion parameters but four times more training text, outperformed models several times larger (Hoffmann et al. 2022). The recipe changed from “make it bigger” to “balance size and data.”

The empty right side of Figure 2 is itself part of the history. As language models became products, the information that would let outsiders judge them (size, training data, compute) largely disappeared from public view. The GPT-4 technical report, for example, gives no parameter count (OpenAI 2023). Since then, most of what we know about frontier models comes from benchmarks, which, as my post on vibe coding discussed, have their own problems.

From predicting text to answering people (2022–2023)

A model trained only to continue text is not an assistant. Asked a question, it may continue with more questions. The step that turned language models into assistants was fine-tuning them on human preferences: people wrote examples of good answers and ranked the model’s outputs, and the model was then trained to produce the kind of answers people preferred, a method known as reinforcement learning from human feedback. In OpenAI’s InstructGPT study, people preferred the answers of a 1.3-billion-parameter model trained this way to those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3 (Ouyang et al. 2022).

On 30 November 2022 OpenAI released ChatGPT, a conversational model trained with the same methods, as a free research preview (OpenAI 2022). An analyst note estimated that it reached 100 million monthly users by January 2023, which would make it the fastest-growing consumer application recorded at the time (Hu 2023). Within a year the field had changed shape:

  • GPT-4 (14 March 2023) handled images as well as text and, according to OpenAI, scored around the top 10% of test takers on a simulated bar exam (OpenAI 2023).
  • Claude, Anthropic’s first public model, was released the same day (Anthropic 2023).
  • LLaMA (February 2023) and Llama 2 (July 2023) from Meta made capable models available as open weights, and Llama 2 was free for research and commercial use (Touvron et al. 2023; Meta 2023). An ecosystem of open models grew around them.
  • Gemini (December 2023) was Google’s first model designed from the start to work with text, images, audio and video together (Google 2023).

Thinking longer, and acting (2024–2025)

Researchers had noticed that language models solve harder problems when prompted to write out their reasoning step by step, a technique called chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al. 2022). In September 2024 OpenAI’s o1 built this into training: the model was trained with reinforcement learning to produce a long internal chain of reasoning before answering, and it performed far better on mathematics and programming problems (OpenAI 2024). In January 2025 the Chinese company DeepSeek released R1, a reasoning model trained in a similar way, with its weights openly available; its Nature paper reported that the reinforcement-learning stage cost about US$294,000 in rented computing time, although that excludes the much larger cost of training the base model it started from (Guo et al. 2025). Reasoning was no longer the preserve of a few companies.

The next step was to let models act. Instead of answering a question, an agent is given a goal and tools (a terminal, a browser, a code editor) and works toward the goal over many steps, checking its own results. Anthropic released Claude Code, an agent that reads, writes and runs code in a developer’s own environment, for general use in May 2025 (Anthropic 2025), and similar tools followed from other companies. METR’s measurements, discussed in my earlier post on writing and coding with LLMs, suggest that the length of task such agents can complete on their own has been doubling every few months (Kwa et al. 2025). The vibe coding of my previous post is a direct product of this period.

AI for science, and for the Earth

The same years brought AI into the natural sciences, in ways that matter more to a hydrology lab than any chatbot. AlphaFold 2 predicted the three-dimensional structure of proteins from their amino-acid sequences with accuracy competitive with experiments, solving a problem that had been open for fifty years (Jumper et al. 2021). In 2024 the Nobel Prize in Chemistry went half to David Baker for computational protein design and half to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper for protein structure prediction, and the Nobel Prize in Physics to John Hopfield and Geoffrey Hinton for foundational work that enabled machine learning with artificial neural networks (Nobel Prize Outreach 2024a, 2024b).

Weather forecasting was transformed next. GraphCast, trained on decades of reanalysis data, produced ten-day global forecasts in under a minute and outperformed the world’s leading operational deterministic forecast on 90% of 1,380 verification targets (Lam et al. 2023). Its probabilistic successor GenCast beat the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts’ ensemble on about 97% of 1,320 targets (Price et al. 2025).

Hydrology moved too. A single LSTM network trained on 531 catchments at once outperformed conceptual models calibrated for each catchment individually, and learned catchment similarities that matched hydrological understanding (Kratzert et al. 2019). In 2024 Grey Nearing and colleagues showed that a global AI model could predict extreme floods in ungauged basins up to five days ahead with reliability similar to or better than the current global system’s same-day nowcasts, and the model now feeds a free public flood-warning service in more than 80 countries (Nearing et al. 2024). The question that Hsu, Gupta and Sorooshian raised in 1995, which Part 1 described, is now being asked of operational systems.

Where things stand in late 2026

The pace has not slowed. In June 2026 Anthropic released Claude Fable 5, which it described as state-of-the-art on nearly all the benchmarks it tested (Anthropic 2026a). On 22 September came Claude Opus 5.5, which Anthropic says performs at the level of its top model, Claude Fable 5.1, on most work while costing 40% less to run than Opus 5 (Anthropic 2026b). On 30 September Google announced Gemini 4 Argon, its new frontier model, reporting a state-of-the-art score on the DeepSWE software-engineering benchmark; it reached the Gemini app on 13 October (Google 2026). OpenAI’s GPT-5 family continued in parallel, and in August 2026 OpenAI described ChatGPT as having more than a billion weekly active users (OpenAI 2026).

All of these claims come from the companies themselves, and none of the models’ sizes, training data or full evaluations are public. That is the honest summary of where the story stands: the systems are more capable than ever, more widely used than any previous technology of their kind, and harder for outsiders to examine than at any point in this history.

What ninety years teach

Turing’s question has not gone away. In 1950 Turing replaced “can machines think?” with a test of behavior. Today’s models pass versions of that test daily, and the argument has moved, exactly as it did after Deep Blue, to what passing the test does and does not show.

Rules lost to learning, but not completely. The learning tradition that began with McCulloch, Pitts and Rosenblatt won decisively. Yet the most useful systems of 2026 are learned models that use rule-based tools: they call calculators, run tests and follow procedures. The two traditions of Part 1 have merged.

Data and computing power decided the timing. Almost every key idea in this series was older than the moment it worked. What changed, again and again, was the amount of data and computing power available to make it work at scale.

Measurement is the bottleneck. From the imitation game to ImageNet to today’s agent benchmarks, each era has had to invent a way to measure progress, and each measure has eventually been outgrown or gamed. For hydrologists, who have argued about model evaluation for decades, this is familiar ground, and it is where our field can contribute.

Bottom line

  • From 2018, pre-training on large amounts of text, with no labels, became the basis of language AI.
  • Scaling laws (2020) made progress predictable; models grew about 2,500-fold in five years until Chinchilla showed that data mattered as much as size.
  • Fine-tuning on human preferences turned language models into assistants; ChatGPT (2022) brought them to the public, followed by GPT-4, Claude, LLaMA and Gemini in 2023.
  • Reasoning models (2024–2025) and coding agents (2025) extended what the models can do on their own.
  • AI transformed protein science and weather forecasting and is entering operational flood forecasting; the 2024 Nobel Prizes recognized both the methods and their scientific use.
  • In late 2026 the frontier includes Claude Opus 5.5 and Gemini 4 Argon, but most of what we know about such models comes from their developers.

Questions to think about

  1. Predicting the next word turned out to require a great deal of knowledge about the world. Is predicting tomorrow’s streamflow from yesterday’s weather the same kind of problem? What would a “pre-trained” hydrological model learn?
  2. Model developers stopped publishing model sizes in 2023. What would you need to know about an AI model before trusting it in an operational flood warning system?
  3. Looking back over the three parts of this series, which idea do you think took longest to go from proposal to practical use, and why?
