Heat and the mind: what we know about climate change and mental health

Created on October 22, 2026

2026   ·   climate-change   mental-health   heat   health   public-health   ·   paper-highlights

When we talk about the health effects of heatwaves and cold surges, we usually mean the body: heat stroke, heart attacks, hospital admissions among older people. These effects are well documented. Many young and healthy people therefore conclude that extreme weather is not their problem. They stay indoors, turn on the air conditioner, and wait for it to pass.

But heat also acts on the mind, and the mind shapes everything we do. Picture a pitcher on a scorching afternoon. The arm may be fine while judgment and temper are not. This side of climate change is harder to measure than mortality or crop yields, and it is less well understood. An umbrella review by Joaquim Radua and colleagues in World Psychiatry is one of the best attempts so far to sort out what we actually know (Radua et al. 2024). This post summarizes it.

A pitcher on a hot day

The baseball example is not hypothetical. Larrick and colleagues analyzed 57,293 Major League Baseball games and asked when pitchers hit batters (Larrick et al. 2011). After controlling for other factors, the probability of a hit batter rose sharply at high temperatures when more of the pitcher’s own teammates had been hit earlier in the game. The authors suggest that heat makes people more likely to read hostility into others’ actions and lowers their inhibitions against retaliating.

Note what this study shows and what it does not. It shows that heat changes behavior that depends on judgment and self-control, in skilled, healthy, highly motivated professionals. It does not show that fastballs get slower on hot days because of mental state; I know of no study that separates the mental and physical sides of pitching performance in the heat. That gap is typical of the whole field.

Why mental health is hard to put numbers on

Mental health outcomes range from a bad mood to suicide, and most of them never appear in any database. Researchers therefore rely on what is recorded: suicides, hospital admissions, emergency visits, diagnoses, or survey scales. Individual studies often disagree. One finds that fine particulate matter is linked to depression, another with a larger sample finds no link; one finds that hot days raise suicide mortality, another does not replicate it. Even meta-analyses contradict each other.

An umbrella review goes one level up. Radua and colleagues collected every systematic review with meta-analysis on air pollution or climate change hazards and mental health published up to June 2023: 32 meta-analyses covering 284 individual studies and 237 exposure–outcome associations. Most associations (195) concerned air pollution; 42 concerned climate hazards, and 35 of those were about temperature.

They then graded each association with standard credibility criteria. To be “convincing” (class I), an association needs more than 1,000 cases, a p-value below 10⁻⁶, no sign of small-study effects or excess significance bias, a prediction interval that excludes the null, low heterogeneity, and a significant result in the largest study. “Highly suggestive” (class II) relaxes some of these requirements. Most associations failed even the weaker tests (Figure 1).

What holds up: heat and suicide

The strongest climate findings concern short-term heat, meaning temperature over days to weeks (Figure 2a). For every 1°C increase in temperature:

  • deaths from suicide or mental disorders rise by 2.4%;
  • suicidal behavior (deaths, attempts, ideation or self-harm) rises by 1.2%;
  • hospital admissions or emergency visits for suicidal behavior or mental disorders rise by 1.1%, and for mental disorders alone by 0.9%.

Apparent temperature, which combines air temperature with humidity to express how hot it feels, shows a similar link with suicidal behavior (1.0% per 1°C). All five reach class II, and all five survive a sensitivity analysis restricted to longitudinal studies, in which the exposure clearly comes before the outcome.

One climate hazard besides temperature also reached class II: people more recently exposed to a cyclone had more severe post-traumatic stress symptoms. That finding rested only on cross-sectional studies, however, and did not survive the longitudinal analysis.

(b) The mortality estimate scaled with land warming since the pre-industrial period, as in the paper’s complementary analysis. The paper estimates about 5% today and about 7% by 2050; the markers sit where the curve reaches those values. Figure by Daeha Kim, drawn from numbers reported in Radua et al. (2024).

Small effects, large numbers

A 1–2% increase per degree sounds small. The authors’ point is that it applies to the whole population on every hot day. To show what this could mean, they combined the mortality estimate with the rise in global land temperature since 1750–1799. By their calculation, deaths from suicide or mental disorders are already about 5% higher than in a pre-industrial climate, and will be about 7% higher by 2050 (95% CI 4.2–9.7%) if land temperatures keep rising at the recent rate (Figure 2b).

This projection should be read as an illustration, not a forecast. It applies a risk estimated for day-to-day temperature swings to a slow rise in the annual mean, and it assumes the relationship is linear and that people do not adapt. Each of those assumptions could be wrong in either direction.

Not only the vulnerable

The review does not analyze age, but other studies speak directly to young and healthy people.

In the summer of 2016, researchers followed 44 university students in the Boston area, with a mean age of 20, through a heatwave (Cedeño Laurent et al. 2018). Twenty lived in buildings without air conditioning, where indoor temperatures averaged 26.3°C; the others lived in air-conditioned buildings at 21.4°C. During the heatwave, the students without air conditioning took 13.4% longer to respond on a color-word attention test and 13.3% longer on simple arithmetic, relative to the air-conditioned group. On the attention test, performance was best at indoor temperatures of 22–23°C. Staying indoors only protects you if the indoor air is cool, and buildings designed to keep heat in during winter can stay hot long after a heatwave passes.

Climate change also affects young people’s minds through worry rather than heat. In a survey of 10,000 people aged 16–25 in ten countries, 59% said they were very or extremely worried about climate change, and more than 45% said their feelings about it negatively affected their daily life and functioning (Hickman et al. 2021). The review mentions this kind of distress (eco-anxiety, ecological grief, solastalgia) but could not grade it, because no meta-analyses of it existed yet.

What the review cannot tell us

The authors are frank about the limits, and they are worth repeating:

  • The underlying evidence is weak. Every one of the 32 meta-analyses was rated low or critically low in methodological quality, mostly for poor handling of heterogeneity and incomplete reporting.
  • Association is not causation. Exposures such as heat and air pollution occur together, and age, sex and income may confound or modify the links. The review could not separate them.
  • Cold is an open question. No association with cold reached high credibility, but the authors note that this does not rule out harm from extreme cold, which has been reported in Northern Europe. For cold surges, the evidence is thinner than for heat.
  • Thresholds are unknown. Effects were analyzed per degree. Whether risk rises sharply above some temperature, as it does for physical health, was not tested.

Air pollution, the other half of the review, produced the only convincing association: long-term exposure to solvents is linked to 14% higher odds of dementia or cognitive impairment. Class II links included carbon monoxide and nitrogen oxides with dementia, particulate matter during pregnancy with postpartum depression, and sulfur dioxide with schizophrenia relapse.

What a hydrometeorologist takes from it

Humidity matters for the mind, too. Apparent temperature, which depends on humidity, carried a link with suicidal behavior as strong as air temperature. Heat warnings based on air temperature alone may miss humid days that feel worse than they read.

Indoor climate is part of exposure. Weather stations measure outdoor air, but people experience the indoor temperature of their homes, classrooms and offices. Linking urban climate and building data to health records would sharpen these estimates a great deal.

Heat warnings could include mental health. Heat-health warning systems already target older people and outdoor workers. The evidence here suggests that mental health services and suicide prevention belong in those plans as well.

Bottom line

The umbrella review confirms that climate change affects mental health, not only physical health. The most credible link is between hot days and suicide, deaths from mental disorders, and psychiatric hospital visits: roughly 1–2.4% more per 1°C. The effects are small for any one person but apply to everyone, every hot day, and they reach young and healthy people too, through slower thinking, shorter tempers and worry about the future. The evidence base is still weak, and questions such as cold, thresholds and causation remain open. Mental health deserves a place in how we measure the human cost of a warming climate.

Questions to think about

  1. In the pitcher study, the heat effect was tied to provocation. What does that suggest about how heat interacts with stress at work or at home?
  2. How would you design a study to separate the mental and physical effects of heat on athletic performance?
  3. Korean summers are hot and humid, and winters bring cold surges. Which data would you need to test whether cold affects mental health outcomes in Korea?
